The Mullah

Verses 37 to 42

Who holds the gate
The Mullah, who speaks for the mosque and the faith
How it is refused
By argument and mockery
Course thread
Refusing religious authority; religion and material power

Verse by verse

  1. 37Ranjha answers the Mullah sharply. The podcast reads Waris Shah here as an iconoclast, exposing the hypocrisy of those who speak for organised religion.
  2. 38The Mullah attacks Ranjha.
  3. 39Ranjha answers with rhetorical questions.
  4. 40The Mullah lectures, claiming authority on the law and the power to guide the faithful to paradise.
  5. 41Ranjha mocks his greed, accuses him of profiting from funerals and of using the law to shield the wealthy and powerful, and rebukes him for turning travellers away from a mosque.
  6. 42The Mullah grudgingly lets him stay the night and warns him to leave at daybreak. Waris Shah steps in to lament the outwardly pious hypocrites who fill the houses of God.

The exchange, in paraphrase

The MullahI know the law of the faith here. I can lead the faithful to paradise.
RanjhaYou make money out of funerals, and you use the law to protect the powerful.
RanjhaA mosque should shelter a traveller, not turn him away.
The MullahFine. Stay the night, and leave at daybreak.
Waris Shah, the poetHe grieves that outwardly pious hypocrites, with no compassion, now fill the houses of God.

My listening note

Hearing the Punjabi recitation before the English translation made me notice how much of Ranjha's defiance lives in his vocal swagger and the rolling cadence of the baint metre. It makes the Mullah sound pompous while Ranjha sounds fearless.

From the course texts

  • Manzur Ejaz, Waris Shah: The IdeologueReads Heer through land, class, religion, patriarchy and material power. He asks what kinds of authority the characters resist, and what keeps drawing them back into the social order. His reading is strongly materialist and sometimes forceful. The instructor adds that Ejaz's Heer videos omit and stress things according to his own ideological position.
  • Najm Hosain Syed, Recurrent Patterns in Punjabi PoetryRanjha at the mosque is the outsider meeting an institution: the same figure Syed traces across the tradition, set against the social order.
  • Farina Mir, The Social Space of LanguageShows qissas living through print, performance and popular piety under colonial rule, and Punjabi cultural life often exceeding the sharper religious and linguistic boundaries that reform movements and colonial institutions promoted.
  • Punjabi poetic forms handoutPlaces Waris Shah's Hir in baint, a major narrative metre closely tied to the qissa. The handout's advice for listening: notice pace, line length, rhythm and pauses, and do not worry about the rules yet.

Verse by verse

  1. 43Ranjha reaches the Chenab, across from Jhang Sial, and meets Luddan. The podcast reads the whole Luddan chapter as comic relief.
  2. 45Luddan refuses passage to those who cannot pay and warns Ranjha against forcing his way on.
  3. 46Ranjha plays his flute on the bank. Luddan's wives are moved by it, and Luddan accuses him of trying to seduce them.
  4. 47Luddan's uproar leads Ranjha to prepare to swim across, and the wives urge that he be welcomed.
  5. 49The wives bring Ranjha aboard, where he is shown to a bed reserved for Heer.

Ranjha(He plays his flute.)

From the course texts

  • Anne Murphy, Locating a Punjabi ClassicTreats Hir as a Punjabi text deeply connected to wider worlds: Persian, other South Asian traditions and different religious vocabularies. For Murphy, Punjab is something made through movement, borrowing and overlap, not a sealed cultural space. A crossing at the Chenab is a good place to read that.

The Five Pirs

Verses 80 to 82

Who holds the gate
Five of the best known Sufi saints of Waris Shah's time
How it is refused
It is not. This door opens.
Course thread
Refusal is selective

What happens

  1. 80Heer reassures Ranjha, who comes from a proud and affluent clan and is unlikely to take well to servitude.
  2. 81 to 82Ranjha meets the five most storied Sufi saints of the time. They declare Heer his divinely ordained companion and tell him to petition them in times of trouble. Their gifts mark him with both status and piety.

  • Anne Murphy, Locating a Punjabi ClassicPoints to Heer's links with different religious vocabularies. The saints at this door speak in one of them, and the Mullah at Door 2 speaks in another.

The Village

Verses 86 to 93

Who holds the gate
Family honour, held by Kaido, Chuchak, Malki and the village
How it is refused
By Heer herself, with force and threats
Course thread
Patriarchy, honour and a daughter's voice

Verse by verse

  1. 87Heer catches up with Kaido and throws him to the ground.
  2. 88She threatens him and demands he return the choori he took from Ranjha, afraid it would be evidence of her feelings for him.
  3. 89Kaido takes his accusations to the village council.
  4. 90Chuchak, whose family honour has been challenged, answers angrily and dismissively.
  5. 91The village women complain to Malki. The podcast calls this commentary on patriarchy, honour and the control of "wayward daughters".
  6. 92Kaido urges Malki to punish Heer, suggesting absurdly harsh punishments.
  7. 93Malki, in a rage, sends for Mithi to bring Heer. The podcast hears a menace of violence beneath the talk of honour and shame.

From the course texts

  • Sara Kazmi, Radical Re-tellings of HirLooks at how Amrita Pritam and Nasreen Anjum Bhatti return to Hir in very different postcolonial settings. Heer's voice, and the older qissa itself, become resources for challenging patriarchy, male literary authority and dominant versions of Punjabi history, including under Partition, nationalism and authoritarian rule. The village's reaction here is the older voice they take up again.
  • Manzur Ejaz, Waris Shah: The IdeologueHis lens on patriarchy and material power applies directly: Kaido, Chuchak and Malki defend honour, and Heer pushes against it.

What refusal looks like in Heer

Refusal in this course is not one gesture. The instructor's brief names refusal, departure, withdrawal, disobedience, world-making and ways of living otherwise, and this road shows several of them inside a single qissa. Ranjha's first refusal is to leave. Then he talks back, he plays music, he accepts a blessing from some authorities, and he watches Heer refuse with her body and her voice.

Three things stand out. First, refusal has targets. The Mullah is mocked and the five saints are honoured, so Waris Shah goes after hypocrisy, not faith. Second, refusal often asks an institution to be what it claims to be. Ranjha does not reject the mosque; he holds it to being a sanctuary. Third, refusal has a cost and a pull. Ejaz asks what keeps drawing the characters back into the social order, and at Door 5 the answer is honour policed by a whole village, with the menace of violence underneath.

Syed finds the outsider recurring across Punjabi poetry, so Ranjha's refusals belong to a long pattern and not a one-off. Kazmi shows later poets taking up Heer's voice again, which is why these refusals keep being retold.

What the road shows

Read across the five doors.

Refusal has targets

The Mullah is mocked and the boatman is outplayed, but the five saints are not refused at all. Waris Shah challenges hypocrisy, not religion as such.

Refusal takes many forms

Leaving, argument, music, force. No single gesture stands for refusal in the qissa.

The poet picks a side

At the mosque, Waris Shah steps in himself. The refusal belongs to the poem as well as to its characters.

Doors this road does not reach

The qissa has many more face-offs. A critic lists these among them.

  • Ranjha and Guru Balnath, where he gives up his name and lineage to become a jogi

Hear the same story in different metres

From the poetic forms handout. Play an example of each and notice pace, line length, rhythm and pauses.

Baint

A major narrative metre tied to the qissa. Punjabi baint developed considerably beyond its Persian antecedents. Waris Shah's Hir.

Dohra

A compact metre, commonly analysed as 13 + 11 matras a line. Short, balanced, with a closing quality.

Davaiyya

A longer metre, conventionally 28 matras, often 16 + 12. Damodar's Hir.

Kali

A kavishri unit tied to Malwa traditions and to Babu Rajab Ali. "Heer di kali" is among the examples.
